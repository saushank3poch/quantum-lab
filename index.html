Could we predict a molecule’s behaviour before making a material?

  1. Ask a useful question. How strongly will these atoms bind? That can help researchers decide which materials to test.
  2. Model their electrons. Electrons follow quantum rules. Some interacting systems are expensive to calculate accurately. Ordinary computers use approximations that already work well for many problems.
  3. Use a quantum processor for difficult parts. Prepare a quantum model, change it, and measure repeatedly to estimate an energy. An ordinary computer helps organise and interpret the work.

    How can the rule recognise an answer we haven’t found?

    Testing a proposed answer can be easier than finding one—like testing whether a key fits a lock. In this search, the rule can recognise a match. Its quantum version changes the matching amplitude’s sign without returning the box number.

    Our simulator defines a hidden target to build that rule. The purple outline is a teaching cue that lets us inspect its effect. It is not a visible label a real quantum computer can simply read.

    Compare the totals and chances

    This advantage applies to this kind of search with a suitable quantum checking rule. Building that rule, preparing the state, other gates, and correcting errors all add work. Fewer rule uses does not automatically mean a faster real chip.

    How the comparison is calculated · optional

    Exactly one uniformly hidden target among N candidates. The classical procedure scans in order and confirms its match: average (N + 1)/2 checks, worst case N. If the last candidate were inferred without verification, that procedure could save one check on the last-target case. This is not a comparison with indexed or sorted databases.

    The quantum model starts with equal amplitudes and applies Grover’s phase oracle and diffusion k times, where θ = asin(1/√N) and k = floor(π/(4θ)). It samples one outcome and spends one additional rule call verifying it. Success probability is sin²((2k + 1)θ). Retrying prepares a fresh state; cumulative calls remain counted.

    FIRST PRINCIPLES · BUILD THE IDEA FROM THE GROUND UP

    What makes a computer quantum?

    Start with three actions: store something, change it, read it. Then change the rules for what can be stored and how it combines.

    No prior physics needed. We start from the quantum rules tested in experiments; we do not derive why nature follows those rules.

    1. A bit2. A quantum state3. Why signs matter4. More qubits5. Useful work
    01

    A computer needs something it can change.

    An ordinary chip uses physical states, such as two voltage ranges, to represent 0 and 1. One of these two-valued units is a bit. Many bits can encode a number, a photo, or an instruction.

    Stored bit 0Read it now: 0.

    Computation means applying rules to stored information. A flip changes 0 to 1, or 1 to 0. A long sequence of simple operations can do useful work.

    02

    A qubit still gives one answer when read.

    A qubit is a physical quantum system with two chosen reading outcomes, labelled 0 and 1. It can be prepared in a superposition of 0 and 1, with contributions to both outcomes that can later interfere.

    To predict what it does, we track a number called an amplitude for each possible reading. In this lab those numbers can be positive or negative. Squaring an amplitude gives the chance of that reading.

    +0.707… amplitude → 50% chance−0.707… amplitude → 50% chance

    So an amplitude is more than a probability. Its sign can change the outcome of a later operation, even when the chances look identical right now.

    The + and − signs are a simple case of phase. General quantum states use complex numbers; these examples only need real numbers. Each box below is a possible answer for one qubit, not a separate qubit.

    03

    Signs matter when amplitudes combine.

    The blue operation combines the two amplitudes. Equal signs can add; opposite signs can cancel. This is interference. Try the same sequence with one change in the middle.

      ONE QUBIT · TWO POSSIBLE ANSWERS

      These numbers are the simulator’s view. A hardware reading gives one answer, not both amplitudes.

      How blue calculates the new amplitudes · optional

      If the incoming amplitudes are a and b, blue makes the new ones (a + b)/√2 and (a − b)/√2. Purple changes b to −b. The √2 keeps the total probability at 100%. These are ideal H and Z gates.

      Why this is different from a random bit: both +/+ and +/− give 50/50 readings immediately, but blue can tell them apart: one becomes certain 0, the other certain 1. A classical 50/50 probability list has no sign relationship to preserve.

      04

      More qubits create more possible readings.

      1 qubit0 · 12 possible readings
      2 qubits00 · 01 · 10 · 114 possible readings
      3 qubits000 · 001 · … · 1118 possible readings

      A general quantum state has an amplitude for each possible bit string. Operations change these amplitudes together. Some joint states are entangled: they cannot be described as an independent state for each qubit.

      But one reading still returns just one string. Reading three qubits might give 101. It does not return all eight possibilities. More amplitudes alone do not make a useful algorithm.

      05

      The job is to make useful answers likely.

      A quantum algorithm must connect the problem to operations that make interference useful. In our search example, a rule recognises a correct candidate and reverses its amplitude’s sign. A second operation combines amplitudes to raise its chance. Repeat the right number of rounds, then read one candidate and check it.

      With 16 candidates and one hidden match:

      An ordinary scan averages 8.5 checks. This ideal quantum search uses 3 rounds + 1 final check, with a 96.1% chance of success per attempt. A miss means more work.

      As this kind of search grows, classical checks grow roughly with the number of candidates; quantum rounds grow roughly with its square root. That is a reason quantum algorithms can be powerful for particular problems.

      This counts uses of a suitable checking rule, not chip speed. Preparing states, building the rule, other gates, retries and error correction also cost work. Ordinary chips remain essential, and quantum computing does not speed up every task.

      A place to understand it.

      Start with an ordinary bit, then predict and test one quantum idea at a time. The beginner path waits for your actions; nothing advances on a reading timer. Equations are optional. Deep Dive keeps the original three animated circuit tours and their detailed controls.

      How much of it is real

      Computed: real-valued statevectors, X, H and Z gates, CNOT, a phase oracle and diffusion, squared-amplitude probabilities, measurement collapse, and independently sampled shots. The carrier’s bars come directly from the current state. Our displayed bit order is q0 q1.

      Scaled down: circuit lessons use one or two qubits; the search comparison simulates 2–8 qubits, 4–256 candidates and one target. It counts checking-rule calls, including final verification and retries, against a classical sequential scan. Each sample represents a fresh circuit run. Beginner experiments use one or 40 fresh runs. Deep Dive uses 1,024. Repeated runs are computed together instead of animating every trip. The molecule example is explanatory, not a chemistry simulation.

      Assumed: ideal initialization and gates; Z-basis measurement. Optional readout noise flips each output bit independently with the chosen probability. It does not model gate errors or decoherence. Sampling uses browser pseudorandom numbers.

      Visual metaphor: buildings, moving carriers, travel time, and the layout are invented. Qubits do not travel through a town. Amplitude bars are simulator information that a single hardware measurement cannot reveal. Only real-amplitude gates are included. Oracle construction and hardware runtime costs are omitted.

      Keep these distinctions

      Negative amplitudes are not negative probabilities. Matching Bell outputs alone do not prove entanglement, and entanglement cannot send a controllable message faster than light. Quantum computers do not let us read every answer at once.
